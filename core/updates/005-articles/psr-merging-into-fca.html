For the past decade, UK payments have had two main regulators. The Financial Conduct Authority (FCA) authorises and supervises payment firms, while the Payment Systems Regulator (PSR) oversees the payment systems themselves. That split is coming to an end.

What has been decided

The government announced in March 2025 that it would abolish the PSR and merge its functions into the FCA, as part of a wider push to simplify financial regulation. HM Treasury consulted on the details in September 2025 (A Streamlined Approach to Payment Systems Regulation) and has since published its response confirming the plan.

Under the plan, the FCA takes on the PSR's functions, including oversight of payment systems, competition and access. It will also get statutory objectives equivalent to the PSR's: promoting effective competition, fostering innovation, and making sure payment systems work in the interests of the people and businesses that use them. Legislation is needed to complete the transfer formally.

Which systems are affected?

The PSR regulates the payment systems designated by HM Treasury. Today these include Faster Payments, Bacs, CHAPS, LINK, Visa, Mastercard and the cheque clearing systems. Oversight of all of them will move to the FCA.

Is anything changing day to day?

The operational merger is already under way, with senior leadership working across both organisations. When the merger was confirmed, the PSR was expected to keep operating as a separate body until at least the end of 2026, so that ongoing work and investigations could continue without disruption.

What it means for businesses

  • One regulator, not two. Payment firms should face fewer overlapping requests and a single point of contact for both firm-level and system-level rules.
  • A more joined-up view. Industry bodies, including retailers, hope one regulator will look across payment systems, services and firms together, instead of making piecemeal interventions.
  • Continuity of protections. An accountability framework will apply to the FCA when it acts on payment systems, replicating the substance of the PSR's current framework.

For merchants, the practical effect in the short term is limited: your card and bank payments work exactly as before. Over time, it should mean clearer, more consistent rules on access, competition and fees across the systems you rely on.
